Intramuros: a walk through Manila's old walled city

How to spend a day in Intramuros, Manila's Spanish-era walled city: Fort Santiago, San Agustín Church, kalesa rides and nearby Binondo.

Ask a Filipino family about Intramuros and someone will remember a school field trip: a line of kids in white uniforms walking under the stone gates of Fort Santiago, a teacher pointing out the brass footprints that mark José Rizal's last walk, and a stop for halo-halo on the way home. Inside the old walls, the noise of modern Manila drops away. Horses' hooves click on the cobblestones, church bells ring across the plazas, and it is easy to imagine the city as it looked in faded sepia photos.

Most international visitors rush through Manila on the way to the islands. Give Intramuros one unhurried day and you will understand the country's story far better before you head to the beach.

What Intramuros is

"Intramuros" means "within the walls." This was the Spanish colonial city of Manila, founded in 1571 and enclosed by thick stone walls that still wrap around much of the old center. For more than three centuries, it was the seat of government, the church and the wealthy families of the colony. Today it is a quiet historic district of about 0.67 square kilometers, cared for by a government agency, the Intramuros Administration.

The best things to see

Fort Santiago

The old citadel at the northern tip of the walls, beside the Pasig River. National hero José Rizal was held here before his execution in December 1896, and a small shrine tells his story. Look for the bronze footprints set into the ground that trace his final walk to Bagumbayan, today's Rizal Park. There is a modest entrance fee.

San Agustín Church

Completed in the early 1600s, San Agustín is the oldest stone church in the Philippines and one of the Baroque Churches of the Philippines listed by UNESCO. It survived earthquakes and the Second World War when almost everything around it fell. Step inside to see the painted trompe-l'œil ceiling, then visit the museum in the old monastery next door.

Manila Cathedral

The cathedral has been rebuilt several times after fires, earthquakes and war. The current building dates from the 1950s and faces Plaza Roma, a pleasant place to sit in the shade.

Casa Manila

A re-created Spanish-era townhouse furnished with antiques, capiz-shell windows and a courtyard. It is the easiest way to picture daily life for a wealthy family in old Manila.

The walls themselves

You can walk sections of the ramparts, especially near Baluarte de San Diego and the gates. Late afternoon is the best time, when the light turns gold and the heat eases.

How to get around

  • On foot: Intramuros is compact. Comfortable shoes and water are all you need.
  • By kalesa: horse-drawn carriages wait near Fort Santiago and Manila Cathedral. Agree on the route and price before you climb in.
  • By bamboo bike: guided tours on bicycles with bamboo frames are a popular, gentle way to cover more ground.

Next door: Binondo

Cross the Pasig River to Binondo, founded in 1594 and often called the oldest Chinatown in the world. Come hungry: dumplings, hopia pastries and fried siopao are all part of the walk. Pair a morning in Intramuros with lunch in Binondo and you have a perfect day.

Memory laneThen and now

Before the Second World War, Intramuros was a living neighborhood of churches, convents, schools and family homes, and older Manileños still speak of it as the most beautiful part of the city. The Battle of Manila in February 1945 left it almost completely in ruins. Only San Agustín Church survived standing. For decades, the old city was a place of rubble and memories. Since the late 1970s, restoration has slowly brought back walls, gates and streets, and many buildings you see today are careful reconstructions. Walking here is a small act of remembering, and it is moving to see how much has been rebuilt.

Practical tips

  • When to go: early morning or late afternoon. Midday heat can be intense, especially from March to May.
  • Getting there: Grab is the easiest way from Makati or the airport. Allow extra time for traffic.
  • Dress: cover shoulders and knees if you plan to go inside the churches.
  • Opening hours for museums and Fort Santiago can change, and some sites close on Mondays. Check before you go.
