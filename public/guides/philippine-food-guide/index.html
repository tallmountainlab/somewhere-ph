Ask anyone who grew up in a Filipino home about food and you will hear about a kitchen, not a restaurant. A pot of adobo simmering on the stove, the smell of garlic rice in the morning, a lola insisting you have not eaten enough, and the call of the taho vendor outside, "Tahooo!", walking down the street with two buckets balanced on a pole. Filipino food is comfort food in the truest sense. It tastes like home, even to people visiting for the first time.

Dishes to try first

Adobo

Often called the national dish, adobo is chicken or pork, sometimes both, braised in vinegar, soy sauce, garlic, bay leaves and black pepper until tender. Every family has its own version and is sure theirs is the best. It tastes even better the next day.

Sinigang

A sour soup, usually soured with tamarind, with pork, shrimp or fish and vegetables such as kangkong (water spinach), radish and long beans. On a rainy day, there is nothing more comforting.

Lechon

Whole roasted pig with crackling skin, the centerpiece of fiestas, weddings and birthdays. Cebu is especially famous for its lechon, stuffed with herbs and so flavorful it needs no sauce.

Kare-kare

Oxtail and vegetables in a thick peanut sauce, served with bagoong, a salty fermented shrimp paste that brings the dish to life.

Sisig

Chopped, crispy pork, often served sizzling on a hot plate with onion, chili and calamansi. It is closely linked with Pampanga, a province known as a capital of Filipino cooking.

Pancit and lumpia

Noodles, or pancit, are served at birthdays for long life. Lumpia are spring rolls, either fried and crisp or fresh and soft. Both arrived with Chinese traders and are now completely Filipino.

Sweets and snacks

  • Halo-halo: the name means "mix-mix." Shaved ice, sweet beans, jellies, fruit, leche flan, ube and evaporated milk, piled high in a tall glass. Stir it all together before you eat.
  • Taho: warm silken tofu with syrup and sago pearls, sold in the morning by street vendors.
  • Turon: banana and jackfruit rolled in a wrapper, fried and coated in caramelized sugar.
  • Ube: purple yam in jams, cakes, ice cream and pastries.

Where to eat

  • Carinderias: small, simple eateries with trays of home-style dishes. Many are "turo-turo," which means "point-point": you point at what you want.
  • Markets: some markets have paluto stalls where you buy fresh seafood and have it cooked next door.
  • Family restaurants: for sit-down meals of shared dishes, perfect for trying many things at once.

How Filipinos eat

Meals are shared. Dishes go in the middle of the table, and rice comes with almost everything, including breakfast. Many people eat with a spoon and fork, using the spoon like a knife. For a festive meal, try a kamayan feast, where food is laid out on banana leaves and everyone eats with their hands. A version called the "boodle fight" comes from a military tradition of sharing one long table of food. Snack time, or merienda, happens mid-afternoon, so do not be surprised to be offered food again.

Christmas food

The Philippines celebrates one of the longest Christmas seasons in the world. During the nine dawn masses of Simbang Gabi, from December 16 to 24, vendors outside churches sell bibingka, a rice cake baked in a clay pot with coals above and below, and puto bumbong, purple sticky rice steamed in bamboo tubes and topped with butter, sugar and grated coconut. If you visit in December, follow the smell of the charcoal.

Memory laneThen and now

Filipino food is a story of every ship that ever docked in the islands. Long before the Spanish arrived, people were already cooking with vinegar, salt and coconut, ways of keeping food fresh in a hot climate, which is one reason adobo and kinilaw, fish cured in vinegar, are so old. Chinese traders brought noodles, spring rolls and soy sauce. More than three centuries of Spanish rule added names and fiesta dishes like leche flan and lechon, and the American years added sandwiches, canned goods and a love of sweet spaghetti. Today, Filipino cooking is having its moment around the world, but its heart is still the same as in a lola's kitchen: a big pot, a lot of rice and the question "Kumain ka na?" Have you eaten yet?

Practical tips

  • Condiments: on most tables you will find vinegar, soy sauce, calamansi and chili. Mix your own sawsawan, or dipping sauce.
  • Water: drink bottled or filtered water, and ask whether ice is made from purified water.
  • Adventurous eating: balut, a fertilized duck egg, is a famous street snack. Try it if you dare.
