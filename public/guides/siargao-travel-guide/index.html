Siargao travel guide: surf, lagoons and island life

Siargao was the kind of place people found by accident, or by rumor. Surfers in the late 1980s and early 1990s came looking for a wave they had heard about and stayed far longer than they planned. The wave got a name, Cloud 9, and a photo of it in a surf magazine changed the island forever. Today you will find cafés and yoga classes, but ride a scooter past the endless coconut palms toward Pacifico and it still feels like that first summer, when nobody was in a hurry.

Getting there

Siargao's Sayak Airport has flights from Manila, Cebu and Clark. You can also fly to Surigao City and take a ferry across. From the airport, it is about 30 to 45 minutes by van to General Luna, where most visitors stay.

Where to stay

  • General Luna is the hub, with the most restaurants, surf schools and nightlife along Tourism Road.
  • Near Cloud 9 is ideal if you want to surf at dawn.
  • Pacifico and Burgos in the north are quieter, with uncrowded surf and fewer services.

Best things to do

  • Island hopping to Naked Island, Daku and Guyam, usually as a half-day or full-day boat trip from General Luna.
  • Sugba Lagoon near Del Carmen, for paddleboarding and jumping off the diving board into green water.
  • Magpupungko Rock Pools, which are best at low tide. Check the tide table before you go.
  • Surf lessons at beginner-friendly breaks. Cloud 9 itself is a powerful reef break for experienced surfers.
  • Coconut Road and the Maasin River swing, best explored by scooter.

When to go

Siargao faces the Pacific, so its weather differs from the west of the country. Calmer, drier conditions usually run from roughly March to October, with the best-known surf swells from about August to November. December to February can be wetter and windier. Typhoons are possible in the second half of the year.

Getting around

Most visitors rent a scooter. Wear a helmet, avoid riding at night on unlit roads, and make sure your travel insurance covers motorbikes. Habal-habal (motorbike taxis) and tricycles are easy alternatives.

Memory laneThen and now

The first surf visitors slept in simple nipa huts and ate whatever the fishermen brought in that day. There was no airport traffic and no Tourism Road, just a few dirt tracks and a lot of patience. Typhoon Odette hit the island hard in December 2021, and the way the community rebuilt says everything about the place. The island has grown up, but the spirit of those early surf days, barefoot, salty and generous, is still very much alive.
