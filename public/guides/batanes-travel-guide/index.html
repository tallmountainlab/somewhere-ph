Batanes: stone houses, rolling hills and the edge of the Philippines

How to get to Batanes, what to see on Batan and Sabtang islands, when to go around typhoon season, and what makes Ivatan life so special.

There is a picture of the Philippines that many people carry in their heads from old postcards and calendars: green hills rolling down to a rough blue sea, a stone cottage with a thick grass roof, cows grazing on a cliff top and not a single tall building in sight. That picture is Batanes. The northernmost province of the country feels like a place where time slowed down on purpose, and the people who live there, the Ivatan, have kept it that way.

Batanes is a group of small islands in the far north, closer to Taiwan than to Manila. Getting there takes planning and some luck with the weather, and that is exactly why it still feels untouched.

Getting there

The usual way in is a flight from Manila to Basco, the provincial capital on Batan Island. Flights are limited and depend heavily on weather, so book early and leave a spare day or two in your plans in case of delays or cancellations. A few routes and schedules change from season to season, so check the latest before you plan.

Batan Island

North Batan

  • Naidi Hills and the Basco Lighthouse: a white lighthouse on a grassy hill above town, best at sunset.
  • Racuh a Payaman: rolling communal pastures that visitors nicknamed "Marlboro Country" after the old cigarette ads, with cows and horses grazing above the sea.
  • Valugan Boulder Beach: a shore covered in smooth, round boulders thrown out by an ancient volcanic eruption, with waves crashing in from the Pacific.
  • Vayang Rolling Hills: sweeping views along the coast.

South Batan

  • Ivana: a village with an old stone church and the famous Honesty Coffee Shop, an unstaffed little store where you take what you need and leave the payment in a box.
  • Old stone houses: scattered through villages such as Ivana and Uyugan, with thick walls of stone and lime and roofs of cogon grass.

Sabtang Island

From Ivana port, a small boat called a faluwa crosses to Sabtang in roughly half an hour to an hour, depending on the sea. The crossing can be rough and is canceled when waves are high. Sabtang is home to some of the best-kept traditional villages, especially Chavayan, with its rows of stone houses, as well as dramatic coastal viewpoints. Most visitors go on a day trip with a local guide and tricycle.

Getting around

On Batan, most visitors join a tour by van or tricycle, or rent a bicycle or motorbike. Roads are good but winding. Tours usually split the island into a northern day and a southern day.

When to go

Batanes sits in the path of many typhoons. The calmest months are usually around March to May, with clearer skies and more reliable flights and boats. From roughly June to October, typhoons are more likely. From about November to February, the northeast winds bring cool, grey and windy weather. Whenever you go, keep your plans flexible.

Memory laneThen and now

For centuries, the Ivatan lived with the wind and the sea. Their stone houses, built low and thick, with heavy grass roofs, were designed to stand through typhoons. Women wore the vakul, a headdress woven from palm fibers, to protect themselves from sun and rain while working in the fields. The islands were annexed by Spain in 1783, and for a long time they were reached only by boat, a trip that could take days. Even today, Batanes feels apart from the rest of the country. Crime is very low, people greet strangers on the road, and the Honesty Coffee Shop still runs on trust. In a fast-changing world, that may be the most nostalgic thing of all.

Practical tips

  • Cash: bring enough pesos. ATMs are limited and can run out.
  • Weather buffer: do not book a tight connection home. Flights and boats are often delayed by weather.
  • Respect: ask before entering private yards or photographing homes and people.
  • Layers: it can be windy and cool, especially on the hills.
