Traveling to the Philippines for the first time

Entry rules, money, SIM cards, getting around and the mistakes first-time visitors make in the Philippines.

Ask anyone who grew up in the Philippines, or who has family there, and they will tell you about the balikbayan box: the big cardboard box packed with chocolates, canned goods and shoes, taped shut and shipped home with love. Ask a first-time visitor what they remember, and it is usually the same feeling in reverse: being welcomed like family. This guide is here to help that first trip go smoothly, so you can spend less time worrying about ferries and more time saying yes to a second plate of lumpia.

Entry basics

  • Visa-free stays. Citizens of many countries, including the United States, Canada, the UK, Australia and most of Europe, can enter visa-free for a short tourist stay, typically 30 days. Extensions are available from the Bureau of Immigration.
  • eTravel registration. All arriving travelers must complete the free eTravel form online shortly before their flight. Use only the official government site and ignore paid look-alike sites.
  • Onward ticket. Airlines and immigration officers may ask to see proof of a return or onward flight, so have it ready.
  • Passport validity. Your passport should be valid for at least six months beyond your arrival date.

Money

The currency is the Philippine peso (PHP). Cards work in malls, chain hotels and city restaurants, but cash is king on the islands, where ATMs can run empty on weekends and holidays. Withdraw enough in Manila, Cebu or Puerto Princesa before heading somewhere remote, and carry small bills for tricycles, tips and environmental fees.

Staying connected

Globe and Smart are the two main mobile networks. By law, SIM cards must be registered with your ID, so bring your passport when you buy one at the airport. Coverage is good in cities and patchy on small islands. An eSIM set up before you fly saves time on arrival.

Getting around

  • Domestic flights are the fastest way to hop between island groups. Philippine Airlines, Cebu Pacific and smaller carriers serve most tourist hubs.
  • Ferries and fast craft connect nearby islands. Schedules change with the season and the weather, so build in a spare day before any international flight.
  • Grab works in Manila, Cebu and other big cities for rides and food delivery.
  • Tricycles and vans handle the last mile in smaller towns. Agree on the fare before you get in.

When to go

The driest, most reliable months for most of the country are roughly December to May, with March to May being the hottest. June to November brings the southwest monsoon and typhoon season, with cheaper rooms but a real risk of cancelled boats and flights. Some places, like Siargao, follow a slightly different pattern, so check each island guide.

Five mistakes to avoid

  1. Trying to see too many islands. Two or three bases in two weeks is plenty.
  2. Booking a tight connection between a ferry and an international flight.
  3. Running out of cash on an island with one ATM.
  4. Skipping reef-safe sunscreen. Several marine areas ask visitors to use it.
  5. Forgetting that Holy Week and Christmas are peak domestic travel periods, when prices rise and seats sell out.

Memory laneThen and now

Not so long ago, getting around the islands meant a paper ticket, a crowded pier and a long wait on a wooden bench while someone sold boiled peanuts and warm Coke in glass bottles. Families brought pillows for the overnight ferry and claimed a spot on deck. Some of that is gone: you can book most flights and many boats on your phone now. But the jeepneys still wear their chrome horses, the sari-sari store on the corner still sells single sachets of shampoo, and a stranger will still walk you to the right bus. The best parts have not changed at all.
