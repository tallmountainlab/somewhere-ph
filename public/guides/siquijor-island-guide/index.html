Siquijor: waterfalls, old churches and the island of fireflies

How to get to Siquijor, how to explore the island by scooter, the best beaches and waterfalls, and the real story behind its mystical reputation.

Ask a Filipino grandmother about Siquijor and you might get a raised eyebrow and a story. For generations, the small island was the setting of whispered tales about healers, love potions and things that go bump in the night, the kind of stories told on a dark porch during a brownout, with a candle on the table and everyone pretending not to be scared. Visit today and you find something gentler: quiet roads under acacia trees, a turquoise waterfall, an old stone church and sunsets that turn the whole sea pink.

Siquijor is one of the smallest provinces in the Philippines, an island in the Central Visayas east of Negros and south of Cebu. It is small enough to circle in a day and calm enough that many people stay much longer than they planned.

The island of fire

When Spanish sailors first passed Siquijor, they are said to have seen the trees glowing at night with swarms of fireflies and named it "Isla del Fuego," the island of fire. On a dark night, away from the towns, you can still see them flickering in the mangroves and along the roadsides.

Getting there

Most visitors come through Dumaguete on Negros, which has an airport with flights from Manila and Cebu. From Dumaguete's port, fast ferries cross to Siquijor town or Larena in roughly 1 to 1.5 hours. There are also ferry routes from Cebu and from Bohol, but they take longer and run less often. Sea conditions can change schedules, so give yourself a buffer before an onward flight.

Getting around

A paved ring road of roughly 70 kilometers loops around the island, and the easiest way to explore is by rented scooter. If you do not ride, hire a tricycle and driver for the day. Traffic is light, but watch for dogs, chickens and kids on bikes, and avoid riding after dark on the quieter stretches.

What to see and do

Cambugahay Falls

Near Lazi, a short flight of steps leads down to Cambugahay Falls, a set of terraced pools of milky turquoise water. Swing from the rope and drop into the pool, or simply float. Come early in the morning, before the tour groups arrive.

Lazi Church and Convent

The coral-stone church of San Isidro Labrador and its enormous convent across the road date from the late 1800s. The convent is often described as one of the largest in Asia. Walk the wide wooden floors and look out at the old trees in the courtyard.

The old balete tree

Between towns stands a huge, twisting balete tree, often said to be several centuries old, with a spring at its roots. Visitors sit on the edge of the pool and let small fish nibble their feet.

Beaches and sunsets

  • Paliton Beach near San Juan: a small strip of white sand with palm trees, best at sunset.
  • Salagdoong Beach in Maria: clear water and concrete platforms for cliff jumping.
  • Tubod Marine Sanctuary in San Juan: easy snorkeling right from the shore.

Where to stay

San Juan is the most popular base, with beach resorts, cafés and the best sunsets. Siquijor town is handy for the port, and the east coast is quieter, with a few hideaway resorts. Book ahead around Holy Week and the Christmas holidays.

When to go

The dry months, roughly December to May, are the best time for clear skies and calm seas. From about June to November, expect more rain and the chance of typhoons, which can cancel ferries. Holy Week is a special time, with local traditions and more domestic visitors.

Memory laneThen and now

Siquijor's mystical reputation has deep roots. The island has a long tradition of folk healers, called mananambal, who use herbs, roots and prayers to treat illness, and Filipino movies and radio dramas of the past turned that tradition into tales of witchcraft and curses. For years, that reputation kept many visitors away. Every Holy Week, healers still gather in the mountain town of San Antonio to collect herbs and prepare their traditional remedies, a custom often called the Lenten Festival of Herbal Preparation. Today, most of the "magic" you will meet is on a souvenir shop shelf, in a small bottle of oil. But sit on a dark beach on a still night, watching the fireflies blink along the shore, and you will understand why the old stories began.

Practical tips

  • Cash: ATMs are limited, so bring enough pesos from Dumaguete.
  • Respect: if you visit a healer, treat it as a cultural visit, ask before taking photos and do not replace medical care.
  • Scooters: wear a helmet and check the brakes and tires before you set off.
