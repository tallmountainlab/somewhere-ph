Some places look like an old photograph even in full color, and Vigan is one of them. Walk down Calle Crisologo in the evening, when the lamps come on, and you hear the clip-clop of a kalesa on the stones before you see it. The wide wooden windows of the old houses are pushed open, capiz shells glowing like paper lanterns. Many Filipino families have a picture of a grandparent or a school trip on this exact street, and it has barely changed since it was taken.

Vigan sits on the northwest coast of Luzon, in the province of Ilocos Sur. It is a long way from Manila, but that distance is part of the reason it survived. Give it a weekend and you will leave with a camera full of photos and a bag of empanada crumbs.

Why Vigan is special

Vigan was founded in 1572 by the Spanish conquistador Juan de Salcedo and grew into a busy trading town, where Chinese, Ilocano and Spanish families mixed and did business. The merchant families built a style of house you will see on every corner: a thick stone ground floor for storage and shops, and a wooden upper floor with big sliding capiz windows to catch the breeze. UNESCO added the Historic Town of Vigan to its World Heritage list in 1999, describing it as the most intact example in Asia of a planned Spanish colonial town.

What to see

Calle Crisologo

The famous cobbled street of old mansions, antique shops and souvenir stalls. It is closed to cars, so you can wander freely. Come twice: early in the morning, when it is empty and cool, and again after dark, when the lamps make it glow.

Plaza Salcedo and the cathedral

The town's main plaza stretches out beside St. Paul's Metropolitan Cathedral, a thick-walled church built in the "earthquake Baroque" style common in the north of the Philippines. In the evening, crowds gather at Plaza Salcedo for a dancing-fountain show set to music. Check the show times locally, as they change.

Bantay Bell Tower

Just outside town in neighboring Bantay, this old brick bell tower once doubled as a lookout for approaching ships and raiders. Climb up for a view over the rooftops and rice fields toward the sea.

Pottery and weaving

At the Pagburnayan pottery area, families still shape burnay, the earthen jars traditionally used to ferment vinegar and fish sauce. You can watch a potter at the wheel and, often, try it yourself. Look also for abel, the Ilocano handwoven cloth, sold as blankets and table runners.

Old houses and museums

Several ancestral homes are open as small museums. The Syquia Mansion is linked to Elpidio Quirino, the Philippine president who was born in Vigan, and is filled with period furniture. Opening hours vary, so ask at your hotel.

What to eat

  • Vigan empanada: a crisp orange shell made with rice flour, filled with green papaya, egg and Vigan longganisa, then fried to order. Eat it hot with spiced vinegar.
  • Vigan longganisa: small, garlicky sausages, perfect at breakfast with garlic rice and an egg.
  • Bagnet: the Ilocano version of deep-fried pork belly, crisp and rich.
  • Okoy: crunchy shrimp fritters, often sold beside the empanada stalls.

Getting there

Vigan is roughly 400 kilometers north of Manila. There are two common ways in:

  • By bus: several companies run direct buses from Manila, including comfortable overnight sleeper services. Expect the trip to take around 8 to 10 hours, depending on traffic.
  • By plane and road: fly from Manila to Laoag, then continue south by van or car for roughly 1.5 to 2 hours.

Once you are in town, everything in the historic center is walkable. Tricycles and kalesas cover the short hops to Bantay and the pottery area. Agree on the price before the ride starts.

When to go

The dry season, roughly November to April, is the most comfortable time, with sunny days and cooler evenings around December and January. From about June to October, heavy rain and typhoons are possible, and roads can be affected. Vigan also holds festivals during the year, including the Longganisa Festival in January and the Viva Vigan Festival of the Arts in May. Dates can shift, so check before you book.

Then and now

Long before cars and buses, Vigan's link to the world was the water. Merchant boats came up the river to the edge of town, and Chinese traders brought porcelain and silk, taking away tobacco, cotton and indigo from the Ilocos fields. The wealth from that trade built the mansions that still line the streets. In the twentieth century, many Philippine towns lost their old centers to war and new construction, and Intramuros in Manila was almost completely destroyed in 1945. Vigan came through largely intact, and families kept living in their grandparents' houses. That is why a stroll here feels like stepping inside a family album: the houses are not reconstructions but the real thing, worn smooth by centuries of footsteps.

Practical tips

  • How long: one full day covers the highlights. Two nights lets you see the town at its calmest, morning and evening.
  • Where to stay: a heritage hotel in a restored house is part of the experience. Book ahead around holidays and festivals.
  • Money: carry some cash for food stalls, kalesas and small shops.
  • Dress: cover shoulders and knees inside the churches.
