If you grew up in the Philippines before 2010, you carried the Banaue rice terraces in your pocket. They were printed on the back of the old twenty-peso bill, green steps climbing a mountainside, and most kids never imagined they could stand in front of the real thing. Then you do. The bus climbs out of the lowland heat into cool mountain air, the clouds lift, and there they are: whole mountains carved into stairs, each level shining with water like a mirror.

The terraces of Ifugao province, in the Cordillera mountains of northern Luzon, are among the most remarkable landscapes in Asia. Getting there takes effort, and that is a big part of the reward.

The terraces in brief

The Ifugao people carved these terraces into the mountainsides by hand and have farmed them for generations, feeding them with water channeled from the forests above. In 1995, UNESCO listed the Rice Terraces of the Philippine Cordilleras as a World Heritage site, covering five clusters, including Batad and Bangaan. The famous viewpoint terraces right above Banaue town are not part of that listing, but they are still the first view most visitors see.

How old are they? Local tradition often says 2,000 years. Some recent archaeological research suggests many were built more recently, within the last few centuries. Either way, they are a living landscape, still planted and harvested today.

Banaue

Banaue is the hub town, with guesthouses, small restaurants and transport to the villages. The main viewpoint is a short ride above town, where you will find a row of souvenir stalls and elders in traditional dress who may pose for photos for a small tip. Ask before you take a picture. Banaue is a good place for your first night and for arranging onward rides and local guides.

Batad: the amphitheater

Batad is the highlight. Its terraces curve around a natural bowl, forming the shape of a giant amphitheater, with the village tucked at the bottom. There is no road into the village itself. From Banaue, a jeepney or tricycle takes you up to the trailhead, and from there you walk down on a steep path for roughly half an hour to an hour. Plan to stay at least one night in a simple Batad guesthouse, so you can see the terraces at sunrise when the mist is still in the valley.

Tappiya Falls

From Batad village, a trail along the terrace walls leads to Tappiya Falls, a tall waterfall with a cold pool at the bottom. Allow around an hour each way. The walk follows the narrow stone edges of the terraces, so take your time and wear shoes with grip.

More villages to explore

  • Bangaan: a small traditional village in a terraced valley, easy to see from the road between Banaue and Batad.
  • Hapao: wide, stone-walled terraces in Hungduan, with a hot spring nearby.
  • Mayoyao: more remote, with fewer visitors and traditional houses among the terraces.

A local guide makes these walks easier and supports the community. Your guesthouse can usually arrange one.

When to go

The terraces change color with the farming year. Roughly speaking, they are flooded and mirror-like after planting, bright green as the rice grows, and golden before harvest. Exact timing differs from village to village and year to year, so ask locally before you go if you are hoping for a particular color. The rainy season, roughly June to October, brings mud, slippery trails and occasional landslides that can block roads. Drier months are easier for hiking. At any time of year, nights are cool, so bring a warm layer.

Getting there

Most travelers take an overnight bus from Manila to Banaue, which usually takes around 9 to 10 hours. Some buses continue toward Sagada, so check your stop. You can also break the trip with a stay in Baguio or combine Banaue with Sagada, a few hours away.

Then and now

For centuries, the terraces were tended family by family, with rituals for planting and harvest and wooden rice gods, called bulul, guarding the granaries. In the twentieth century, a newer road reached Banaue and visitors began to arrive, and the terraces became a national symbol, printed on money and in schoolbooks. Then came hard years. Many young people left for jobs in the cities, some terraces fell into disrepair, and from 2001 to 2012 UNESCO listed the site as World Heritage in Danger. Restoration work and community programs helped it come off that list. Today you will still see families working the paddies by hand, and when you walk along a terrace wall at dawn, you are following paths that their grandparents' grandparents walked.

Practical tips

  • Cash: bring enough pesos. ATMs in Banaue can be unreliable, and Batad has none.
  • Respect the walls: walk on the edges carefully and never step into the planted paddies.
  • Pack light: you carry everything down to Batad and back up again.
  • Signal: mobile coverage can be weak in the valleys.
